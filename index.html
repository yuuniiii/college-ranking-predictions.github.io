Motivation and Background

Every year, high school seniors fall down the YouTube rabbit hole when deciding which college to attend. Dorm tours and day-in-my-life vlogs seem like obvious primary sources to evaluate a potential university. But does any of it actually reflect reality?

This project investigates whether the narratives projected about colleges on social media align with their actual prestige. We are particularly interested in whether content creators from elite schools benefit from a “prestige safety net” that allows their content to lean on intangibles such as campus culture and aesthetics, while non-elite schools feel more pressure to advertise aspects more directly related to ROI.

Research shows that social media increasingly shapes how students choose colleges. A 2025 survey found that 60% of students use social media to research colleges, with YouTube being one of the most used platforms. Another study found that 69% of applicants used social media to research postsecondary institutions primarily to understand campus life and student experience.

A 2026 study in the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science analyzed over one million social media posts from UK universities. It found that elite schools gain more engagement from posts about campus aesthetics and facilities, while non-elite schools perform better when emphasizing student support and personal interaction.

Existing literature has focused primarily on university recruitment strategy and brand equity, not on whether the themes in college-related content systematically vary by prestige tier. This is the gap we aim to investigate.

Social media increasingly functions as a college search engine for prospective students.

Methodology Overview

We frame prestige prediction as a binary classification task: predicting whether a university belongs to the “elite” category.

We compare two modeling approaches:

  • Institutional-data-based baseline model
  • YouTube transcript-based NLP model

Institutional features were sourced from the U.S. Department of Education’s College Scorecard dataset, while prestige labels were derived from U.S. News rankings.

We scraped YouTube transcripts using yt-dlp and constructed NLP pipelines using TF-IDF, logistic regression, engineered sentiment features, and Bidirectional LSTMs.

We compare structured institutional information against social-media-derived language features.

Baseline Model Architecture

Data

We merged College Scorecard institutional data with U.S. News rankings, defining elite institutions as schools ranked within the national top 30.

Baseline Model

We constructed an MLP using eleven institutional characteristics and trained using weighted loss to compensate for severe class imbalance.

Elite schools made up only around 10% of the dataset.
Training and validation loss curves for the institutional MLP baseline.

The baseline MLP converges steadily over training epochs, with validation loss decreasing consistently. Mild overfitting emerges later in training, though overall validation performance remains strong.

The model achieves a weighted F1 score of 0.94 and overall accuracy of 0.93 on the validation set.

Institutional variables alone provide very strong predictive signal.

YouTube Model Architecture

YouTube Data Loading and Preprocessing

We sampled 40 schools (20 elite and 20 non-elite) and queried YouTube using prompts such as:

  • "[university] day in my life"
  • "[university] college tour"

We extracted autogenerated transcripts and computed:

  • Word count
  • Sentiment polarity
  • Subjectivity

TF-IDF + Logistic Regression

We applied TF-IDF vectorization to transcripts and trained a logistic regression classifier using stratified 5-fold cross validation.

Bidirectional LSTM

We also implemented a Bidirectional LSTM to capture contextual sequence information beyond bag-of-words representations.

TF-IDF measures how distinctive words are across transcripts.
Performance comparison between transcript-based models.

Performance

The TF-IDF-only model achieves 72.0% cross-validation accuracy. Adding engineered sentiment features slightly reduces performance.

The Bidirectional LSTM performs near random chance at 50.8% accuracy, suggesting that deep learning struggled under limited data conditions.

Classical NLP outperformed deep learning on this dataset.
Confusion matrix for TF-IDF logistic regression predictions.

Ablation Study

To determine what drives model performance, we conduct two ablation experiments.

First, transcript length alone achieves only 50.3% accuracy, no better than random chance.

Second, shuffling transcript word order reduces performance from 72.0% to 62.5%, suggesting that phrasing and structure carry signal.

Vocabulary choice matters more than transcript length.

Model Comparison

Our institutional MLP substantially outperforms transcript-based models, achieving a weighted F1-score of 93.9%.

The transcript model reaches 72.0% accuracy, significantly above random chance but well below institutional-data performance.

The performance gap likely represents information contained in structured institutional records but absent from social media discourse.

Institutional data remains far more predictive than online narratives.

Key Analyses, Implications, and Limitations

Do Elite and Non-Elite Schools Talk About Different Things?

Keyword frequency analysis reveals surprisingly mild differences between elite and non-elite school transcripts.

Non-elite schools discuss careers, opportunities, internships, and campus life slightly more frequently, supporting our hypothesis that non-elite institutions emphasize ROI.

The linguistic differences between prestige tiers are weaker than expected.
Keyword frequency comparison between elite and non-elite transcripts.

Limitations

Our dataset is heavily skewed toward schools with larger online presences, introducing selection bias.

Auto-generated transcripts also introduce substantial transcription noise, which likely affected TF-IDF analysis.

Severe class imbalance and uneven geographic representation further constrain interpretation.

YouTube visibility itself may correlate with institutional prestige.

Conclusions, Ethics, and Future Work

Our findings suggest that YouTube content contains some meaningful prestige-related signal, though substantially less than structured institutional data.

Interestingly, simple NLP methods outperform deep learning approaches on this dataset, indicating that scale limitations matter heavily in educational social-media analysis.

Future work could expand the dataset, include additional social platforms, and explore transformer-based architectures such as BERT or RoBERTa.

NLP provides a scalable way to analyze institutional narratives at scale.
